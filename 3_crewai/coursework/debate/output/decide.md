The **opposition** is more convincing.

Here’s why:

The pro side makes a strong case that AI will increasingly take over a large share of coding work. Its arguments about repetitive tasks, cost efficiency, scalability, and the fact that software is already digital are all persuasive. It is very plausible that AI will automate much of the routine production work in software development, and that engineers’ day-to-day roles will shift dramatically.

However, the opposition wins on the key question in the motion: **replace software engineers entirely as a profession**. It argues that software engineering is not just code generation, but also interpretation of messy requirements, negotiation of tradeoffs, accountability for failures, and long-term judgment. Those are not side issues; they are central to what software engineers do in real organizations.

The strongest point for the opposition is accountability. Even if AI can write code, companies still need humans to own decisions, manage risk, and answer for mistakes. In practical terms, that makes full replacement much harder than partial automation. The opposition also rightly points out that AI can be useful without being reliable enough to be entrusted with the whole engineering function end-to-end.

The pro side is strongest when arguing that the profession will be fundamentally changed, and it is convincing that many engineering tasks will be automated. But it overreaches when it treats that as proof of full replacement. The evidence presented supports **major disruption and augmentation**, not complete replacement of software engineers as a profession.

So, based on the arguments presented, **the opposition is more convincing** because it better explains why AI may transform software engineering without fully replacing the human engineer.
