I oppose the motion.

**Strict laws to regulate LLMs are the wrong answer because they would punish the safest, most useful part of the technology while failing to stop the real harms.** LLMs are general-purpose tools, like search engines, word processors, or the internet itself. If you regulate the tool too harshly, you don’t just restrain bad actors—you also burden doctors, teachers, small businesses, researchers, and ordinary users who benefit from it every day.

Here’s the core problem: **strict laws sound protective, but in practice they usually create overreach, confusion, and gatekeeping.** LLMs evolve quickly. Hard-coded legal rules will be outdated almost immediately, forcing companies into compliance theater instead of real safety improvements. Worse, big firms can afford the legal teams and audits; startups and independent developers cannot. So “strict regulation” often ends up entrenching the biggest players and killing competition, which is the opposite of a healthy innovation ecosystem.

Second, **the harms attributed to LLMs are already addressed by existing law.** Fraud, defamation, privacy violations, impersonation, and cybercrime are illegal whether an AI is involved or not. If someone uses an LLM to scam, threaten, or deceive, punish the conduct—not the entire technology. We do not ban printing presses because people can print propaganda. We enforce the laws against the harmful act itself.

Third, **strict regulation risks suppressing free expression and beneficial use.** LLMs help people write, translate, code, summarize, brainstorm, and access information. Heavy legal restrictions can push providers toward censoring legitimate content out of fear, reducing usefulness for everyone. A model that is too tightly constrained becomes less capable, less open, and less accessible.

The better approach is **targeted, flexible oversight**: enforce existing laws, require transparency where necessary, and hold bad actors liable for actual harm. That protects the public without turning a powerful tool into a legal minefield.

So the question is not whether LLMs should be used responsibly—they should. The question is whether we want blunt, strict laws that slow down progress and entrench incumbents, or smart enforcement that punishes misuse without strangling innovation. The answer is clear: **do not impose strict laws on LLMs.**
